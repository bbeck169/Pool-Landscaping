# Module A — Filter Cleaning · Chapter 2: Pressure diagnosis and clean-cycle timing

*Original in-house authorship. External standards cited by name + link only.*

## The pressure gauge is your primary instrument

Every filter housing has a pressure gauge. That gauge, compared to the *clean* pressure recorded when the filter was last serviced, tells you when to clean. Rule of thumb across all media types: when current pressure exceeds clean baseline by 8–10 psi, clean the filter.

## Clean baseline — record it every time

At the end of every filter cleaning, note the pressure with all valves in normal operating position and pump running at design speed. Write it on a waterproof sticker inside the equipment pad door, or log it in the service report. Without a clean baseline, you're guessing.

## Time-based schedules are a backup, not primary

Many services default to a monthly filter clean regardless of pressure. That's fine as a safety net for pools with unreliable gauges or absent baselines, but it misses two failure modes: 1) a fouled skimmer basket that mimics filter loading (pressure high, filter actually clean), and 2) a channel or crack that keeps pressure low while allowing debris through.

## Diagnosing "high pressure but filter looks clean"

1. Skimmer basket full or restrictor plate missing.
2. Main drain plumbed closed or shifted valve position.
3. Filter media caked into a hard crust (sand) — needs deep clean, not just backwash.
4. Return-side plumbing partially blocked (rare — check heater bypass and salt cell).

## Diagnosing "low pressure with dirty water"

1. Cracked lateral (sand filter) — bypass channel.
2. Torn grid fabric (DE filter).
3. Cartridge folded/wadded in housing — pleats not sealing on standpipe.

## When pressure spikes suddenly overnight

Usually algae bloom sending organics into the media. Confirm with a free-chlorine test and a visual pool check before assuming a mechanical problem.
