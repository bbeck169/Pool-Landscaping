# Module A — Filter Cleaning · Chapter 1: Filter media types and what they trap

*Original in-house authorship. Manufacturers cited by name + external link only. No verbatim OEM manual content.*

## Why the media matters

Filter media is what physically stops debris from re-entering the pool. Different media catch different particle sizes and behave differently under load, so a tech's first job is to identify what's in the tank before making any cleaning decision.

## Sand (silica or glass)

Silica sand #20 grade is the most common residential media. It traps particles down to roughly 20–40 microns. Modern replacements — recycled glass beads or zeolite — are graded finer (10–15 microns) and last longer per pound. Sand beds "channel" as debris accumulates unevenly, so periodic manual raking during a deep-clean cycle keeps the bed even.

## Cartridge (pleated polyester)

Cartridge elements filter to about 10–15 microns and are the norm on newer residential builds because they save backwash water. The pleats trap debris on their surface, so cleaning is a rinse-and-degrease procedure, not a backwash. Elements degrade with UV and repeated compression; expect a 2–3 year service life under normal residential load.

## DE (diatomaceous earth)

DE grids are fabric-covered stainless armatures coated with a slurry of DE powder. That coating traps particles as fine as 3–5 microns — the finest of the three types. DE requires respiratory PPE (N95 minimum) any time you handle dry powder.

## Choosing a cleaning path from the media

- Sand or glass → backwash + occasional deep chemical bath
- Cartridge → rinse + degrease + acid dip (if calcium-scaled)
- DE → backwash + recharge, teardown clean 1–2× per year

## Reading the pressure gauge as the signal

A rising differential pressure (current PSI minus clean-baseline PSI) is the universal signal that the media is loaded. The specific threshold varies by filter type but the diagnostic is media-independent: 8–10 psi above clean = clean the filter now.

## Safety recap

Never open a filter under pressure. Always relieve pressure via the air-bleed valve first, verify the pressure gauge reads zero, and confirm the pump is off and locked out before removing the clamp band.
