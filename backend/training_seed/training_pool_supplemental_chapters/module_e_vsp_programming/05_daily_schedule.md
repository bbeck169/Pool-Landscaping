# Module E — VSP Programming · Chapter 5: Building a daily schedule

**Original in-house authorship. Refer to the OEM manual for the exact steps on any specific model.**

A well-designed VSP schedule has **three or four RPM windows per day**, sized to the duties above. A typical residential pool in a warm climate:

**Window 1 — Overnight low.** 6–8 hours at the lowest RPM that keeps the pool's surface moving and the salt cell producing chlorine at the demand rate. This is the majority of the day's runtime and typically 1200–1500 RPM. Energy cost is negligible.

**Window 2 — Skimming boost.** 1–3 hours mid-morning at a moderate RPM (2000–2500) that hits skimming target GPM. This is when leaves and cottonwood dust land on the water.

**Window 3 — Filter cycle.** 2–4 hours in the afternoon at a moderate-to-high RPM (2500–2800) that hits the turnover flow. On many pools this window and Window 2 collapse into one continuous mid-day cycle.

**Window 4 — Peak / feature.** Optional. Full-RPM for water feature, aggressive filtration, or pool-cleaner runtime. 1–2 hours only. Schedule this outside peak utility rate hours.

Add the four windows and confirm the day's total runtime hits or exceeds the pool's daily turnover requirement.

Salt-water pools have one extra constraint: the salt cell needs a minimum flow to produce chlorine and to trigger the cell's flow switch. That minimum sets a floor on the overnight-low RPM regardless of any other consideration.

Every VSP has a "priming" mode that runs at maximum RPM for a fixed short window when the pump first starts. Leave that enabled. It's what re-primes the volute at the start of each window.
