# Module 3 — Small Engine Repair · Chapter 4: Ignition, spark plugs, and starting systems

*Original in-house authorship. Consult the OEM manual for your specific engine's spark plug part number + air gap.*

## Spark plug replacement

The cheapest, easiest fix; the first thing to try after fuel is fresh.

1. Pull the wire. Wait 60 sec if engine is warm.
2. Remove the plug with a spark plug socket (3/4" or 13/16" on most small engines, model-dependent).
3. Inspect: tan/light-gray insulator = healthy. Black wet = fuel-flooded. Black dry = running rich or oil-fouled. White = running lean or overheated.
4. Gap the new plug to spec (typically .025–.030" on 4-stroke, .020" on 2-stroke).
5. Install hand-tight, then torque per manual. Overtorque cracks the aluminum head threads — $$$$$.

Rule of thumb: replace the plug every 100 hours of runtime or annually, whichever comes first.

## Ignition coil

If a good plug + fresh gap produces no spark, next culprit is the coil (aka magneto). Air gap between coil and flywheel magnet is typically .010–.014" — check with a business card as a rough gauge. Loose coil = weak spark.

Coils fail with age, especially on engines stored in humid environments. Replacement is straightforward — two screws hold the coil to the block.

## Starting systems

- **Recoil starter.** Broken pull cord, worn pawls, cracked pulley. Rebuild kits are $10-20 and take 15 minutes.
- **Electric start (rare on small engines except riders).** Weak battery (charge overnight before condemning starter), corroded solenoid, faulty starter motor.
- **Kill switch grounded.** Some starters won't turn if the kill switch has a shorted wire touching the block. Diagnose by disconnecting the kill wire and trying again.

## Compression relief

Larger engines have a compression release built into the camshaft to make pulling easier. If starting suddenly feels "hard as hell" on an engine that used to pull easy, the compression relief may be stuck engaged. OEM service manual is your guide.
