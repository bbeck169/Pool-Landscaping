# Valve Diagnostics — In-Field Repair and Replacement

**Original in-house authorship. Consult the OEM manual for model-specific torque and rebuild kit specs.**

Once diagnosis points to a specific subsystem, the field repair flow is straightforward. Have the manufacturer's rebuild kit or a replacement valve of the exact matching model on the truck before you commit to opening the valve box.

**Solenoid replacement.** Turn off the water supply upstream of the valve. Unscrew the solenoid by hand — most spin off with only firm finger pressure once loosened with a wrench a quarter turn. Do not use pliers on the plastic body of the solenoid; you will crack the housing. Note the two solenoid wires (typically red and black or both black) and disconnect them from the valve wiring using waterproof gel-filled wire nuts. Screw the new solenoid in hand-tight plus a quarter turn with a wrench; overtightening cracks the port threads. Reconnect the wires with new waterproof connectors — do not reuse old ones. Turn water back on and cycle from the controller to confirm operation.

**Diaphragm rebuild.** Turn off water upstream. Remove the solenoid first, then the four to six top-cover screws (some valves use a bonnet ring instead of screws; check the OEM manual). Lift the cover slowly — the diaphragm and spring will come with it or stay in the body. Note orientation before removal. Inspect the diaphragm for tears, splits, or hardening; inspect the body seat for scoring or pitting. Replace both diaphragm and spring with the matching rebuild kit. Reassemble in the reverse order, tightening cover screws in a star pattern (like a wheel lug pattern) to keep the seal even.

**Full valve replacement.** Sometimes the body is cracked or the threads are stripped past a repair. Shut off water at the mainline. Cut the inlet and outlet PVC unions or cut the pipe cleanly if no unions exist. Install a new valve with new slip couplers or unions (unions on both sides are a courtesy to your future self and to any technician who follows). Use PVC primer and cement per manufacturer's directions. Allow cure time — typically 30 minutes at room temperature — before pressurizing.

Rewire the solenoid using waterproof connectors. If the wire is old and brittle, cut back to fresh insulation before making the connection. Restore water, purge the zone by activating from the controller, and verify no leaks at every joint.

Document the repair in the customer's service record: model number, part numbers used, date, tech initials. Future service calls at the same property are dramatically faster when the last tech left a good record.

Field diagnostic tip: after any valve repair, cycle every zone in the system from the controller, not just the repaired one. A repair often exposes an issue on a neighboring valve that was borderline and gets pushed over by the momentary pressure change of the mainline being restored. Catching that adjacent problem on the same visit turns a callback into a completed service.
