# Pavers & Hardscape — Edge Restraint and Joint Sand

**Original in-house authorship. Vendor products cited by name/link only.**

Two systems hold a paver installation together laterally: the edge restraint that keeps the perimeter from spreading outward, and the joint sand that keeps individual pavers from shifting relative to their neighbors. Skip either and the installation walks itself apart under freeze/thaw or normal traffic within a few seasons.

For edge restraint, you have three common choices. Rigid plastic or aluminum edging spiked into the base rock is the industry-standard solution for straight or gently curved edges — it's fast, invisible once buried, and long-lived when installed with spikes every twelve inches into compacted base. Concrete toe beams are a heavier-duty option for driveways and vehicle-load areas; you pour a small concrete beam along the perimeter after laying pavers but before joint sand, hidden below finish grade so no concrete shows. Natural stone or masonry curbing serves both as edging and as a visible design element.

Whichever you use, the goal is a continuous, stiff perimeter that holds the outer paver course exactly where it landed. A restraint that flexes or breaks contact anywhere along its length will let the outer course start creeping outward and the field will follow.

Joint sand is either standard washed masonry sand or polymeric sand. Standard sand is cheaper and lets pavers shift slightly with the freeze/thaw cycle — good for older, historically inspired installations. Polymeric sand contains a polymer binder activated by water; after installation and light watering, it sets into a semi-rigid material that resists weeds, ants, and washout from garden hoses or heavy rain. Polymeric is the default modern choice for driveways and heavily used patios.

To install joint sand, sweep dry sand across the finished field, working it into every joint until the joints are level with the top of the paver chamfer (the small bevel around each paver's top edge). Use a plate compactor with a protective mat on top of the pavers to vibrate the sand deeper. Add more sand and repeat until joints stop absorbing more. Then sweep the surface clean of loose sand.

If using polymeric sand, follow the manufacturer's activation procedure exactly — under-watering leaves it powdery and it washes out; over-watering floats the polymer to the surface where it dries as a hazy film across the pavers. Test on a small area first.

Field tip: when sweeping joint sand, always work diagonally across the pattern, never straight along the joints. Diagonal sweeping fills the joints from multiple angles and prevents the "top of joint packed, bottom hollow" failure that shows up as a paver rock a year later.
