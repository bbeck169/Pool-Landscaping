# Pavers & Hardscape — Base Prep and Site Layout

**Original in-house authorship. Vendor products cited by name/link only.**

A paver patio or walkway is only as durable as the base underneath it. When a homeowner calls you six months after install with a low spot or a tilted corner, nine times out of ten the failure started here — before the first paver ever went down.

Start every job by walking the site with the customer and marking two things: the finished elevation you're building to, and every drainage flow that has to keep working around your new hardscape. Paint two colors — one for the finished paver edge, one for the underlying excavation edge. The excavation edge is always at least six inches wider than the finish, because your base needs to extend past the load, not stop under it.

Excavate to a depth equal to paver thickness plus one inch of bedding sand plus base rock. On typical residential work in our region, that means about eight inches for a walkway and nine to ten inches for a driveway or heavy-vehicle area. Dig to firm subgrade. If your boot sinks in more than a quarter inch when you stomp, the ground is too soft — either dig deeper until you hit stable material, or plan on adding a geotextile fabric layer to prevent the base from migrating down into the soil.

Slope matters. Every hardscape should drop at least one eighth of an inch per foot away from any structure. On patios adjoining a house, walk this out with a laser or a line and measure it: if you're building a 12-foot patio, you need at least one and a half inches of drop from wall to outer edge. Anything less will pond water against the foundation the first time it rains hard.

Base rock should be a well-graded aggregate — commonly called Class II, road base, or 3/4-inch minus depending on your local supplier. Avoid clean rock (pea gravel, drainage rock) for the base layer; it doesn't compact. Lay the base in two-inch lifts. Compact each lift with a plate compactor until the surface stops deforming under the plate. On residential driveway work you should be able to walk across your finished base and leave no visible footprints.

Screed the bedding sand — always coarse washed concrete sand, never mason sand — to a uniform one-inch depth after compaction. Do not compact the bedding sand; it's meant to receive and cradle each paver. Rescreed anywhere you disturb it.

Field diagnostic tip: any hardscape failure that shows up as a wave, dip, or sunken corner within the first year almost always traces back to skipped compaction lifts or bedding sand thicker than an inch. Get the base right and everything on top of it becomes routine.
