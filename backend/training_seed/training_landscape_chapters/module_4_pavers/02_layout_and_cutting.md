# Pavers & Hardscape — Pattern Layout and Cutting

**Original in-house authorship. Vendor products cited by name/link only.**

Once base and bedding sand are ready, layout is the next skilled step. A good layout hides cuts, keeps courses straight, and creates the illusion of intentional design rather than a filled-in polygon.

Start by choosing a leading edge. On patios that adjoin a house, that leading edge is almost always the wall. On free-standing walkways, snap a chalk line down the geometric centerline and lay the first course from there in both directions. Whichever anchor you pick, that first row is the row every future row will chase — take the extra minutes to get it perfectly straight and perfectly level to your target slope.

Pattern selection depends on paver shape. Running bond (a simple half-lap brick pattern) is fast and forgiving. Herringbone at 45 degrees is more visually complex, resists rutting under vehicle load, and is the pattern of choice for driveways. Basketweave and pinwheel patterns require rectangular pavers of specific dimensional ratios and don't tolerate slight sizing variations well.

Order pavers by area plus a ten to fifteen percent waste factor. Waste covers cuts, breakage, and future repairs. Set aside a full pallet-corner's worth of matching pavers with matching lot numbers and store them at the customer's property — the same lot ensures color consistency if a paver ever has to be replaced.

Cutting is where jobs slow down. Use a wet-cutting brick saw with a diamond blade — dry cutting a paver creates enough silica dust to be a real health hazard, and any inspector or safety-conscious homeowner will notice. Mark each cut with a wax pencil against the paver in place, not on the ground. Cut just outside the line and dress the edge with the blade — better to lose a millimeter twice than to cut a millimeter short once.

Around obstacles like drains, posts, or planter edges, use a template rather than freehand marking. A scrap of cardboard cut to the exact obstacle profile transfers cleanly to a stack of pavers with a wax pencil and gets you consistent cuts across the pattern.

Field tip: if a cut ends up short by more than a quarter inch, do not fill the gap with sand and call it done — pull the paver, cut a fresh one, and reset it. Wide sand gaps are the first thing to weed out in year two, and every one is a warranty callback waiting to happen.
