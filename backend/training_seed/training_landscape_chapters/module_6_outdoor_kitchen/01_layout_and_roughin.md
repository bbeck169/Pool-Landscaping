# Outdoor Kitchen — Site Layout and Rough-In Decision Tree

**Original in-house authorship. Consult a licensed gas/electrical trade for any regulated work.**

An outdoor kitchen install is largely a coordination and layout job. The visible countertop and appliances are the last five percent of the work; the base structure, utility rough-in, and code approvals are the ninety-five percent that determines whether the finished kitchen is functional or a liability.

Start with the site walk. Confirm three utility runs will be available at the finished kitchen location: gas (natural or LP), electrical (typically at least a 20-amp GFCI circuit; often a dedicated 40-50 amp for a refrigerator + rotisserie combo), and water plus drain if the design includes a sink. Mark the utility runs at the customer's meter or panel and pace the distance to the kitchen — long runs increase both material cost and voltage drop / pressure loss concerns that need engineering input.

Confirm the intended cooking surface(s). A single 36-inch gas grill is the minimum footprint most designs support. A grill plus side burner adds roughly 18 inches of counter. A pizza oven or smoker adds 24-36 inches plus separate ventilation clearances. Draw a scale plan showing every appliance's exact model dimensions with the required side clearances (typically 6-12 inches per manufacturer) and clearance-to-combustibles distances above (12-24 inches above the grill hood or higher, depending on canopy material).

Decision tree for the rough-in coordination:

1. **Is a licensed gas/electrical trade doing the utility work?** If yes, get their plan and their permit numbers on the file before you start any framing. Do not frame or pour a base slab without utility rough-in stub locations confirmed on the print. Missed stubs mean saw-cutting finished stone later — an expensive, ugly correction.

2. **Is the kitchen enclosed under a roof or gazebo?** If yes, confirm ventilation. Any gas grill under an enclosed roof needs either an outdoor-rated vent hood (typically 900+ CFM) ducted to outside, or the ceiling above the grill needs approval as an open-air structure with adequate free-air openings per code. This is not optional and not negotiable.

3. **Is there any part of the kitchen touching the home's wall?** If yes, coordinate with a general contractor or engineer. Any structural attachment to the primary building envelope is regulated work.

4. **Is there LP gas involved?** LP tanks must be located outside any enclosed space, at least ten feet from any ignition source per most codes. LP stub-outs into a kitchen island require a specific approved regulator arrangement — this is the number-one code violation we see homeowners try to DIY.

Document every decision in a scaled plan handed to the customer and to any subs. Every subsequent step in the install references this plan.

Field tip: photograph every utility rough-in from three angles before any concrete is poured or framing goes up. The photos become the as-built record for future service calls and for any code inspector who asks how it was installed.
