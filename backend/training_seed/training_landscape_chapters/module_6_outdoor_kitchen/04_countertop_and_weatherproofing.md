# Outdoor Kitchen — Counter Surfacing and Weatherproofing

**Original in-house authorship. Vendor products cited by name/link only.**

Countertop and enclosure are the two surfaces that decide whether an outdoor kitchen lasts five years or twenty. They're also the surfaces homeowners judge the project by. This chapter covers material choices, the templating and install sequence, and the weatherproofing details that separate a lasting install from a callback.

**Material selection.** The dominant outdoor kitchen counter materials are natural stone (granite, quartzite), porcelain slab, poured concrete, and stainless steel. Engineered quartz — the polished polymer-and-quartz composite used indoors — is generally not suitable outdoors: the polymer resin binder yellows and cracks under sustained UV and thermal cycling, usually within two to three years. If a customer insists on quartz, put the risk in writing before you order.

Granite handles heat and UV well. Quartzite (a natural stone, not engineered quartz — different material despite the confusing name) is even more heat and stain resistant. Porcelain slabs are lighter and easier to fabricate, and modern porcelains handle UV and heat without issue; the trade-off is edge chipping if impacted. Poured concrete is highly customizable and warm-looking but must be sealed regularly. Stainless steel is durable and heat-tolerant but shows fingerprints and requires more day-to-day care from the customer.

Match the material to the customer's climate and maintenance appetite. In our region, granite and porcelain are the two most common choices for a reason — they hit the sweet spot of durability, aesthetics, and low maintenance.

**Templating.** Do not template from the plan. Field variance always accumulates through framing, block work, and enclosure fabrication. Template off the completed enclosure, with the appliances (or at least their manufacturer templates) in position. Use a rigid template material — coroplast or hardboard — cut and marked with reference lines to the enclosure edges. The one-hour investment in a good template saves the six-hour fitting session later.

Overhang decisions belong in the templating conversation. A typical exterior overhang on the front is one and a half inches; on the sides, three-quarters to one inch. The overhang has to clear appliance access doors and drawers with room to open them fully. Check every door swing before signing the template.

**Install sequence.** Set the counter on a thin bed of construction adhesive rated for outdoor use — dabs, not a continuous bead. Continuous adhesive traps moisture that then finds its way into the enclosure. Level with shims where needed; a counter slightly out of level will show water pooling within the first rain.

**Weatherproofing details.** These are what fail first when they're skipped:
- Every horizontal joint between counter and vertical face gets a bead of high-quality outdoor-rated silicone (not latex caulk — latex fails in UV within a year). Tool the joint smooth so water sheds off, not pools on.
- Faucet penetrations, sink flanges, and any cutout through the counter get sealed underneath as well as at the topside — water always finds the underside.
- Backsplashes should be tightly caulked at the wall and counter joints, and installed with a slight backward tilt (a couple of degrees) so water drains away from any interior structure rather than pooling at the base.
- The bottom-of-enclosure to concrete-slab joint gets a sealant bead too. Water tracking in through the base joint is a silent killer of enclosure interiors — you won't see it for a year, but the structural framing rots from below.
- Access doors and drawers should be gasketed (most quality outdoor-rated cabinet hardware ships with gaskets) and their sills should slope outward slightly to shed water rather than trap it.

**Commissioning check.** Before you leave, run a spray test with a garden hose held 12 inches above the counter and moved slowly across every joint for ninety seconds. Anywhere you see water intrusion, add sealant now. Ninety seconds of spray at install is worth a hundred hours of trying to diagnose a leak six months later.

Field tip: photograph every sealed joint on install day and store the images tied to the project record. When a customer calls a year later about a leak, those photos tell you whether they compromised your sealant or a joint has failed on its own.
