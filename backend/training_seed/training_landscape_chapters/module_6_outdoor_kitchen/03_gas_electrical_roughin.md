# Outdoor Kitchen — Gas and Electrical Rough-In Expectations

**Original in-house authorship. Consult a licensed gas/electrical trade for any regulated work.**

Rough-in is the coordination point where a good outdoor kitchen becomes possible and a bad one becomes a permanent regret. Once the enclosure is framed and the counter is set, changes to gas and electrical stubs cost five to ten times what they cost during rough-in. This chapter walks the decision tree we follow before any concrete is poured or any structural work begins.

**Step one — appliance list.** Before you talk about lines and circuits, get the customer to commit to the appliance list in writing. Grill, side burner, refrigerator, ice maker, sink, lighting, accessory outlets, and any planned additions for the next two years. Every one of those has a specific electrical or gas requirement, and the difference between a 20-amp circuit and a 30-amp circuit is a decision you make at rough-in, not after.

**Electrical rough-in.** For a basic grill island with lighting and a couple of accessory outlets, a single dedicated 20-amp GFCI-protected circuit is typically the minimum. Add a refrigerator or ice maker and you usually want a second dedicated circuit — mixing an inductive load (compressor start) with lighting on the same circuit causes nuisance tripping every time the fridge cycles. Any island with a rotisserie motor, an outdoor TV, warming drawers, or an outdoor pizza oven typically needs a small subpanel — usually 60-amp or 100-amp, sized by the licensed electrician per the appliance load calculation.

Every outdoor outlet must be GFCI-protected. Any receptacle exposed to weather must be a weather-resistant type with an in-use "bubble" cover. Conduit runs to the island are typically PVC below grade with a metallic sweep at the emergence, and the stub must terminate inside the enclosure at a location that will remain accessible after the counter is set. Photograph the terminated stub from three angles — this is your as-built record.

**Gas rough-in.** Natural gas versus liquid propane is the first decision, and it's usually made by the site: NG if there's a utility connection at the house within a runnable distance, LP if not. The BTU total of the appliance list determines line size — a full outdoor kitchen with a 60,000-BTU grill, a 30,000-BTU side burner, and a 15,000-BTU pizza oven is over 100,000 BTU total, which means the supply line has to be sized (typically ¾ inch or larger) to deliver that flow at the required pressure. Undersized gas lines are the number one cause of an outdoor kitchen that "worked for a month and now the grill can't get hot" — the appliances are fine, they're just starving for pressure.

LP tanks must be located outside any enclosed space, typically at least ten feet from ignition sources per most local codes. Do not plan a tank compartment inside the island body unless it's fully vented outdoors per the manufacturer's spec and your local code allows it. If in doubt, keep the tank in a separate free-standing enclosure adjacent to the kitchen.

**Decision-tree summary.** Ask the customer these five questions before rough-in:
- Full appliance list (final, not aspirational)?
- Distance from house's utility panel and gas meter to the kitchen location?
- Any planned additions in the next two years?
- Roof or shade structure planned overhead (affects ventilation requirements)?
- Local jurisdiction — is a permit required, and what setback rules apply?

The answers determine whether you need one circuit or a subpanel, half-inch or three-quarter-inch gas line, an in-line pressure regulator, and where the utility rough-ins terminate. Get these wrong and no amount of finish work will save the project.

Field tip: on every rough-in job, take a marked-up floor plan into the field with the utility stub locations dimensioned from two fixed reference points (usually the house wall and a property corner). Six months later, when a nearby landscape trench cuts a line, that dimensioned print is what tells the repair crew where to dig.
