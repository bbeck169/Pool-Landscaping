# Outdoor Kitchen — Enclosure, Appliances, and Commissioning

**Original in-house authorship. Consult a licensed gas/electrical trade for any regulated work.**

Once utility rough-in is inspected and approved and any structural attachment is complete, the outdoor kitchen build shifts to enclosure construction and appliance drop-in. Two enclosure approaches dominate residential work: masonry (block or brick with a veneer finish) and metal-stud framing with a cementitious backer board finish. Masonry is more expensive, longer-lived, and heat-tolerant; metal-stud-with-backer-board is faster, lighter, and more common for standard grill islands.

Whichever you choose, the interior of the enclosure needs to be non-combustible where any heat-generating appliance mounts. Manufacturers publish specific enclosure clearance and material requirements per appliance model — read them, follow them, keep the printed pages in the customer file. Grill and burner inserts always ship with a template showing exactly how large the countertop cutout must be and how much clearance to allow beneath and to the sides.

Countertop material is aesthetic plus functional. Granite, quartzite, and porcelain slab are the three commonly specified materials. Quartz (engineered) is generally not appropriate outdoors — the resin binder yellows and cracks under UV and heat exposure. Confirm material selection with the fabricator; templating is done off the finished enclosure, not off the plan, since minor field variance always accumulates.

Appliance drop-in sequence:
1. Confirm every appliance rough-in stub is at the exact position on the fabricator's template.
2. Set the grill or burner into its cutout and verify it sits flush and level.
3. Connect gas per the licensed trade's plan; leak-test with a soap solution or manometer per code.
4. Connect electrical to GFCI-protected circuits.
5. If a sink is included, connect water supply and drain; test for leaks at all joints.
6. Install any lighting or ancillary outlets.

Commissioning is the final walk-through. Fire every burner in every appliance and let it run for at least five minutes. Check for gas smells, unusual soot, or yellow flames — any of which indicate a supply problem that must be corrected before the customer accepts the kitchen. Run the refrigerator for 24 hours before delivery to confirm it holds temperature. Fill the sink and drain it fully to confirm no drain leaks and no slow drain.

Deliver a written commissioning checklist and appliance manuals to the customer. Explain shutoff locations for gas, water, and electrical. Photograph the finished install for the customer's record and yours.

Field tip: keep a portable manometer and a bottle of soap-water leak-test solution in the truck. Most commissioning failures are minor connection leaks that are fifteen-minute corrections when you find them at commissioning and multi-day corrections when the customer finds them a week later.
