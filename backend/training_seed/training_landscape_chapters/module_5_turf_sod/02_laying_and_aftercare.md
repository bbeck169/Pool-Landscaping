# Turf & Sod — Laying, Watering-In, and Aftercare

**Original in-house authorship. Vendor products cited by name/link only.**

Start laying sod along the longest straight edge of the site — a driveway, walkway, or building line. Butt each roll tightly against the previous one; visible gaps invite weeds and dry-out. Stagger the joints between rows like brickwork so no two adjacent seams line up. On slopes greater than roughly one-in-six, lay sod perpendicular to the slope and stake the top edge of each roll with a wooden peg or biodegradable staple to prevent sliding until roots take hold.

Cut around irrigation heads and edges with a sharp sod knife or utility knife. Never overlap sod — an overlap will die back into a visible dead strip within two weeks. When cutting to fit an edge, cut on the underside so the top surface remains uniformly clean.

Immediately after laying, roll the finished surface lightly with a water-filled lawn roller to press the roots into contact with the soil below. Then water — deeply, immediately, and generously. The first watering is the most important one you will ever do on that lawn. Apply enough water that if you lift a corner of sod an hour later, the soil an inch below the sod line is dark and moist. That's roughly half an inch of applied water on a warm-weather install.

For the first two weeks, water twice daily in warm weather, keeping the top inch of soil moist at all times. In cooler weather, once a day is often enough. The sod is drawing water directly from its own mat plus what it can reach in the top inch of soil; the deep root system that lets it survive dry periods hasn't developed yet.

Around day 10-14, tug gently on a corner. Firm resistance means the roots have knit into the underlying soil. At that point, cut back watering to once every other day but increase depth so water penetrates four to six inches. This trains the root system deep.

First mow at three to four weeks, once the sod is firmly rooted. Cut only one third of the leaf blade height. Sharp blade only — a dull mower blade tears sod that has just barely rooted and pulls whole rolls back up. Set the deck high (three inches for cool-season, two to two and a half for warm-season) for the first month; drop it gradually if the customer wants a shorter final height.

Field diagnostic: the first sign of a failed install is a yellowing seam pattern along the roll edges. That's dry-out from gaps or from watering that hits sod tops but not seams. Correct by hand-watering seams for a week and, if necessary, lifting and re-butting the worst rolls.
