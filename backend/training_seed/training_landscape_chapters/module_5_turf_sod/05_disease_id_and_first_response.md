# Turf & Sod — Common Disease Identification and First Response

**Original in-house authorship. Vendor products cited by name/link only.**

When a customer calls about a "dying lawn," the first job isn't a fungicide — it's a diagnosis. Ninety percent of the fungal disease we see in maintained turf traces back to a fixable cultural condition: watering time, watering frequency, mowing height, thatch, or airflow. Treat the condition and the fungus retreats on its own most of the time.

This chapter walks the three symptoms you'll see most often in our region and the decision tree that separates disease from stress.

**Brown patch (Rhizoctonia).** Presents as roughly circular patches of tan-to-brown grass, typically one to three feet across, most often in warm-season turf during warm humid weather. Look for a darker "smoke ring" of water-soaked leaves at the edge of the patch in early morning. Blades pull free easily. First response: shift watering to pre-dawn only (never evening — wet blades at night is the trigger), raise mowing height, and dethatch if thatch is over half an inch. If cultural fix doesn't slow spread within a week, a granular turf fungicide labeled for brown patch is appropriate — but note that untreated cultural cause will bring it back.

**Dollar spot.** Small circular tan patches the size of a silver dollar. Cluster together into irregular areas as the disease progresses. Most common in low-nitrogen turf under dew-heavy morning conditions. First response: apply a light nitrogen feeding per label rate — this alone clears mild cases within two weeks. Water in the early morning so blades dry quickly. Mow with a sharp blade; dull-blade tears leave open wounds that dollar spot exploits.

**Pythium blight (fast).** The one to move on immediately. Presents as small greasy dark patches that grow rapidly — a few feet overnight in the worst cases. Blades feel slimy in the morning; you may see cottony white mycelium in the dew. Triggered by high humidity plus night temperatures above 65 °F plus poor drainage. First response: stop watering immediately, especially at night. Improve drainage in the affected area. A pythium-labeled fungicide is warranted here (unlike the previous two, cultural fix alone is often not fast enough). Reduce mowing traffic across affected turf until the outbreak is contained — you can spread it on mower wheels.

**Decision tree for the field visit.** Walk the site with the customer and note four things: recent watering time, recent watering frequency, thatch depth (push a screwdriver in and look at the vertical profile), and mowing height. In our experience, three out of four "disease" calls resolve to one of these:
- Watering at 7 p.m. or later (blades stay wet all night) — shift to pre-dawn
- Watering every day for 5-8 minutes (encourages shallow roots and constant leaf wetness) — deep and infrequent instead
- Thatch over half an inch (creates a warm, damp microclimate) — dethatch and topdress with compost
- Mowing too short (weakens the plant) — raise the mower

**When to escalate.** If you see spread of more than a few feet per day, unusual grey/black discoloration, or a pattern that doesn't match any of the three profiles above, bag samples in a paper (not plastic) bag and send to your regional extension office or a diagnostic lab. Do not guess with an unfamiliar pathogen; the wrong fungicide can accelerate the wrong disease.

**Callback etiquette.** When you shift watering settings during a diagnosis visit, walk the customer through the controller changes yourself and leave a printed note of the new schedule and the reason for the change. Half of the reinfection cases we see are because someone reset the controller a week later "because it looked dry."

Field tip: your best diagnostic tool is a lawn dew check at 6 a.m. If the customer can send you a phone photo of the affected area at dawn, you can often make the ID before you even arrive.
