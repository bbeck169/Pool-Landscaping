# Chapter 3 — Filtration

*Original in-house authorship. External standards cited by name and link only.*

## What filtration does and does not do

Filters remove suspended solids from the water; they do not disinfect. That distinction matters when techs try to "filter their way out" of a chemistry problem — no amount of extra pump runtime will destroy bacteria, oxidize organics, or lower pH. A filter's job is to keep the water clear enough that sanitizer can reach every bather and every surface, and to keep debris out of the heat exchanger and pump seal.

## Turnover rate and filter cycle

Turnover is the time it takes to pump the entire volume of the pool through the filter once. Residential pools typically target one turnover every 8–12 hours; commercial pools follow their local health code, often 6 hours or shorter for high-bather-load facilities. The filter runs the volume of water through the media multiple times per turnover; a single pass through a sand filter is not enough to clarify contaminated water. When bathers overload a pool, the fix is longer runtime and better skimming, not more chlorine alone.

## Sand filters

Sand filters use a bed of specially-graded silica sand — usually #20 grade — supported by a lateral system inside a fiberglass or steel tank. Water enters through a diffuser, spreads across the top of the sand, and works its way through the bed. Particles as small as 20–40 microns are trapped between the sand grains. As debris accumulates, the pressure gauge on the filter climbs. When it rises 8–10 psi over the clean baseline, it is time to backwash — reversing the water flow to lift debris out of the top of the bed and send it to waste. Modern glass and zeolite media improve the filtration rating to about 10 microns.

## Cartridge filters

Cartridge filters use pleated fabric elements mounted inside a housing. They filter down to 10–15 microns without backwashing, saving water. The trade-off is manual cleaning — every 3–6 months for residential use — with a garden hose and periodic degreasing in a detergent solution. Cartridge filters like a lower flow rate (around 0.375 gpm per square foot of media) than sand and are the wrong choice for a pool that must handle very high bather loads unless the housing is oversized.

## Diatomaceous earth (DE) filters

DE filters use fabric grids coated in a fine layer of diatomaceous earth — the fossilized skeletons of marine microorganisms. The DE traps particles down to about 3–5 microns, giving the finest filtration of the three types. DE filters are backwashed like sand and then require the operator to add a fresh charge of DE through the skimmer to reset the coating. DE dust is a respiratory hazard; wear an N95 respirator when handling it.

## Pressure gauge interpretation

The pressure gauge on the filter is the single most important instrument in the mechanical system. A stable, low reading (usually 8–15 psi depending on plumbing) indicates a clean filter and unobstructed flow. A rising reading means the filter is loading up. A falling reading during operation usually means the pump is losing prime, the skimmer is clogged, or a valve is closed on the suction side. Always compare the current pressure to the *clean* pressure written on the filter label or logged in the service report.

## Backwashing best practice

Backwash a sand or DE filter until the sight glass runs clear — typically 2–4 minutes for sand and 1–2 for DE. Never backwash a cartridge filter; those must be cleaned by disassembly. Backwash water is heavily loaded with organics, chlorine, and phosphates and should not be discharged onto lawns or into storm drains where local code forbids it.

## Filter sizing

Undersized filters are the leading cause of persistent cloudy water. Filter area is expressed in square feet; the manufacturer publishes a maximum design flow rate. A common target is a filter sized 20–30 percent larger than the pump's rated flow. When retrofitting a variable-speed pump, verify that the filter can still handle the pump's maximum flow — a pump that outruns the filter media pushes debris straight through.

## Field practice

Log the clean and dirty pressure readings on every visit. Backwash based on pressure rise, not on the calendar. Inspect the o-rings and multiport valve gasket at every media change. When water clarity is stubborn despite clean filters and good chemistry, the culprit is almost always inadequate turnover, phosphate loading, or hidden algae in dead zones.
