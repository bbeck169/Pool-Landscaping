# Chapter 8 — Regulations and Code

*Original in-house authorship. External standards cited by name and link only.*

## The regulatory landscape

Pool operation in the United States is governed by a patchwork of federal, state, and local rules. Federal rules address the most catastrophic risks — suction entrapment, chemical labeling, workplace safety — while state health departments write the codes that most operators actually enforce day to day. Local jurisdictions add zoning, fencing, and inspection requirements on top. This chapter walks through the important layers and identifies where to find the current text of each.

## The Virginia Graeme Baker Pool and Spa Safety Act (VGB Act)

The VGB Act, signed into law in 2007, requires every public swimming pool and spa in the country to use suction fittings that meet ANSI/APSP/ICC-16 and to include, where required, an anti-entrapment safety device such as a safety vacuum release system, a gravity drainage system, or an unblockable drain cover. The law was named for a young child who drowned as a result of body entrapment on an ordinary flat drain cover; enforcement is delegated to the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC). Operators are responsible for verifying that any replacement drain cover is a current, certified model — an expired cover legally counts as no cover at all.

## ANSI/APSP standards

The Pool & Hot Tub Alliance (PHTA), formerly the Association of Pool & Spa Professionals, publishes a family of American National Standards under the ANSI/APSP/ICC banner. The three most relevant to daily operations are:

- **ANSI/APSP/ICC-11** covers water quality — the target ranges for pH, alkalinity, chlorine, and other parameters that most state codes adopt by reference.
- **ANSI/APSP/ICC-16** covers suction fittings, the technical criteria underlying VGB Act compliance.
- **ANSI/APSP/ICC-7** covers suction-entrapment avoidance — the design and installation requirements for drain configurations that satisfy VGB.

State codes tend to adopt these ANSI standards as a floor, sometimes with tighter local variations.

## The CDC Model Aquatic Health Code (MAHC)

The MAHC is a voluntary, evidence-based model code published by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. States and municipalities may adopt the MAHC in whole, in part, or as a reference document. The MAHC covers everything from design and construction to routine operations and outbreak response, and its fecal-accident response protocols (see Chapter 5) are the industry benchmark even in jurisdictions that have not formally adopted the code.

## Fencing and barrier requirements

Barrier requirements for residential pools are governed by the International Swimming Pool and Spa Code (ISPSC), adopted in most U.S. jurisdictions in some form. Typical requirements include a fence at least 48 inches tall (60 inches for some jurisdictions), openings less than 4 inches, self-closing and self-latching gates that open away from the pool with the latch mounted high enough that a small child cannot reach it, and either a solid-panel wall on the house side of the pool or self-latching pool alarms on any doors leading to the pool deck. Local codes always trump the model.

## Accessibility — the ADA

Public accommodations must comply with the Americans with Disabilities Act. The ADA's 2010 Standards for Accessible Design require most public pools to provide accessible entry — a fixed pool lift, sloped entry, transfer wall, or transfer system — sized and located per the standard. Retrofit deadlines are past; new construction must include accessible entry from day one.

## Local health-department inspection

Most operating public pools are inspected on a schedule by the county or state health department. Inspectors will look for a current operator's license (which is why the operator training you are reading is required), current SDS binder, a functioning test kit, calibrated chemical automation, secure barriers and gates, current VGB-compliant drain covers, and clean daily logs. Failed inspections shut the pool immediately and require a re-inspection before reopening; chronic failures escalate to license suspension.

## Workplace regulation — OSHA

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration governs employee safety at any facility that employs people, including pools. Key OSHA rules relevant to pool operations include the Hazard Communication Standard (SDS binder, chemical labeling), the Personal Protective Equipment standard, and — for commercial facilities — bloodborne pathogen procedures. Operators are responsible for ensuring their staff has training and access to the required PPE.

## Environmental discharge

Backwash water, filter cleanings, and pool drain-downs contain chlorine, phosphates, and (in high-CYA pools) stabilizer that harm aquatic life. Many municipalities require dechlorination before discharge to storm sewers or lawns. Check the local wastewater or environmental services department for the exact rule before draining any pool.

## Field practice

Keep a printed or digital copy of the local code section on-site. Log inspections and correction actions immediately after every visit. When drain covers approach their listed replacement date — usually 5 or 7 years — order replacements before the next scheduled service so they can be swapped in one visit rather than left non-compliant.
