# Chapter 5 — Disinfection and Recreational Water Illness Prevention

*Original in-house authorship. External standards cited by name and link only.*

## Why disinfection is a public-health responsibility

A pool is a shared body of water. Every bather brings in skin flora, sweat, urine, and — occasionally — infectious pathogens. When disinfection fails, the pool becomes a delivery mechanism for outbreaks of gastrointestinal, respiratory, and skin infections. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention groups these outbreaks under the term *recreational water illness* (RWI). Preventing RWI is the single most important reason the pool operator's job exists.

## CT values — the disinfection benchmark

Disinfection depends on both the concentration of sanitizer and the contact time. The product of those two factors, *CT* (concentration × time), is how public-health researchers quantify how quickly a given organism is killed. For example, *E. coli* is killed by free chlorine so fast that the CT is under one minute-ppm at pH 7.5. *Giardia* cysts require closer to 45 minute-ppm. *Cryptosporidium* is a different animal entirely: even at free chlorine of 20 ppm, it takes over ten hours to inactivate — which is why cryptosporidiosis outbreaks continue to occur in properly-chlorinated pools.

## Chloramines and the "pool smell"

The sharp chemical odor associated with indoor pools is not chlorine — it is combined chlorine, mostly trichloramine (nitrogen trichloride) and dichloramine. These irritants are heavier than air and hover at the water surface, exposing swimmers and instructors first. Chloramines cause eye stinging, coughing, and reactive-airways symptoms. The fix is threefold: reduce the amount of nitrogen entering the pool (require pre-swim showers, enforce a no-diapers rule for lap pools), maintain proper ventilation, and reach breakpoint through superchlorination.

## Cryptosporidium and fecal accident protocols

Because chlorine cannot rapidly kill *Cryptosporidium*, the CDC recommends specific fecal-accident response procedures, which are captured in the Model Aquatic Health Code (MAHC). For a formed-stool accident, close the pool, remove the material, and raise free chlorine to 2 ppm at pH ≤ 7.5 for 25 minutes. For a diarrheal accident (higher pathogen risk), the protocol is *hyperchlorination*: raise FAC to 20 ppm at pH ≤ 7.5 and hold for 12.75 hours. Both procedures assume CYA is below 15 ppm — if CYA is higher, dilute the water first, because stabilizer dramatically slows the CT progression.

## Ultraviolet and ozone as supplemental treatments

UV lamps installed in the return line inactivate a wide range of pathogens, including *Cryptosporidium*, in a single pass. UV does not maintain a residual — chlorine is still required — but it dramatically shortens the effective CT time. Ozone is a similar case: strong oxidizer, no residual, requires a chlorine base. Both technologies are supplemental, not replacements.

## Legionella and warm-water systems

Warm water is friendlier to bacteria than cool. Spas at 100–104 °F are a natural environment for *Legionella pneumophila*, the cause of Legionnaire's disease. Spa maintenance requires higher FAC targets (3–5 ppm), constant pH control, and regular drain-and-refills — usually every one to three months, based on the total dissolved solids and bather load. Legionella outbreaks are almost always traced back to biofilm inside the plumbing; regular use of a residual sanitizer is the primary prevention.

## Bather load and turnover math

Bather load is the number of people using the pool per unit time. High-bather-load facilities — public pools, waterparks, condominium pools in the summer — need faster turnover, higher sanitizer targets, and more oxidation capacity than a typical residential pool. The MAHC recommends explicit calculations: designers size the filter for a peak bather load, and operators are expected to close the pool when the load exceeds that number.

## Pre-swim hygiene

The single most impactful RWI-prevention practice is enforcing pre-swim showers. A brief soap-and-water rinse removes 90 percent of the sweat, urine, cosmetics, and skin oils that would otherwise consume chlorine and generate chloramines. In commercial facilities, posted signage and staff enforcement matter more than any additional chemistry.

## Field practice

Test every visit for FAC, CC, and pH. Investigate CC above 0.2 ppm immediately. Verify that any fecal accident is documented, treated per MAHC, and logged. Train pool operators to close the facility if disinfection targets cannot be met — not to hide the problem with more shock.
