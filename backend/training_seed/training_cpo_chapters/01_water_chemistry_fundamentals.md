# Chapter 1 — Water Chemistry Fundamentals

*Original in-house authorship. Cites external standards by name and link only. See end of chapter for links.*

## Why water chemistry drives everything you do

Every service call in the field ultimately comes down to controlling six numbers: pH, total alkalinity (TA), calcium hardness (CH), cyanuric acid (CYA), free available chlorine (FAC), and combined chlorine (CC). If any of those numbers drift far enough, the pool either becomes unsafe, damages itself, or damages the equipment attached to it. Chapter 1 focuses on how the numbers relate to each other so that when you make one adjustment on a Tuesday, you can predict what will happen to the rest of the chemistry by Friday.

## pH — the master variable

pH measures how acidic or basic the water is on a logarithmic scale from 0 to 14. Pools should be maintained in a narrow band, generally 7.2 to 7.8, for two reasons. First, human eyes and mucous membranes are most comfortable at a pH close to the pH of tear fluid (about 7.4). Second, the germ-killing power of chlorine collapses as pH climbs: at pH 7.5, roughly half of the free chlorine exists as the strongly disinfecting form (hypochlorous acid), while at pH 8.2 it is barely 15 percent. When pH climbs above 8, sanitizer effectiveness falls faster than most operators expect.

## Total alkalinity — the pH buffer

Total alkalinity is the sum of dissolved carbonates and bicarbonates and behaves as the pool's pH shock absorber. TA around 80–120 ppm keeps pH stable against small acid or base additions. TA that is too low lets pH swing wildly with every dose; TA that is too high locks the pH near 7.8–8.0 and resists correction. Because TA and pH share the same acid additions to lower them, technicians should always adjust TA first, verify pH afterward, and correct pH only if it strays outside the target band after TA is in range.

## Calcium hardness — protecting surfaces

Calcium hardness measures dissolved calcium ions and controls the water's aggressiveness toward plaster, grout, tile, and heat-exchanger metals. Below about 200 ppm, water hungrily leaches calcium out of the pool surface. Above roughly 400 ppm, it deposits scale on the heater, salt cell, and tile line. Recommended ranges vary between plaster, vinyl, and fiberglass pools; always match the target to the surface installed.

## Cyanuric acid — chlorine's sunscreen

Cyanuric acid (CYA), also called stabilizer, absorbs the ultraviolet energy that would otherwise destroy chlorine within a few hours of sunlight exposure. A CYA level of 30–50 ppm cuts chlorine loss to sunlight to a manageable rate. Above 100 ppm, CYA begins to "over-shield" chlorine — the sanitizer is present, but its killing power drops noticeably because a growing fraction of the chlorine is bound in a slow-release complex. Local codes may cap CYA at 90–100 ppm for public pools.

## Free, combined, and total chlorine

Free chlorine (FAC) is the sanitizer available to disinfect. Combined chlorine (CC) is chlorine that has already reacted with contaminants, forming chloramines that irritate eyes and skin and smell strongly. Total chlorine (TC) is the sum of the two. Aim for FAC in the 1–4 ppm range and CC below 0.2 ppm. When CC rises, the corrective action is superchlorination — driving FAC up sharply to oxidize the combined forms — not adding more slow-release chlorine.

## The Langelier Saturation Index (LSI)

LSI combines pH, TA, CH, water temperature, and TDS into a single number that predicts whether the water will scale or corrode. A slightly positive LSI (0.0 to +0.3) is safe; values above +0.5 tend to scale; below −0.3 they tend to corrode. In the field, LSI thinking is why we do not chase a single number in isolation — cold winter water tolerates far higher pH than warm summer water before it starts to corrode.

## Field practice

For every service visit, take a full test panel before adding anything. Adjust TA first, pH second, calcium and CYA only when trending out of range. Never mix concentrated chemicals in the pump basket or in a bucket; always predissolve calcium hypochlorite and never add water to acid. Retest before you leave the site.
