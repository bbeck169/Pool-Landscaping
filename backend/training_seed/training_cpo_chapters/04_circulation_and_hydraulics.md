# Chapter 4 — Circulation and Hydraulics

*Original in-house authorship. External standards cited by name and link only.*

## The four things a circulation system must do

Every pool's plumbing system has four jobs. It must (1) move all of the water through the filter and heater fast enough to hit the required turnover rate, (2) return that water to the pool in a pattern that leaves no dead zones, (3) protect swimmers from suction entrapment at every drain and skimmer, and (4) do it all quietly enough that the equipment pad does not annoy the homeowner. When any of the four fails, the water either goes untreated in one corner of the pool or the equipment starts to fail early.

## Suction and return sides

The suction side of the system runs from the skimmers and main drain to the pump inlet. Suction pipes must never leak air — a small air leak on the suction side collapses pump prime, cavitates the impeller, and destroys the pump seal within weeks. The return side runs from the pump discharge through the filter, heater, and any auxiliaries, and back into the pool through the return jets. Return-side leaks lose water and chemistry but do not usually endanger the equipment.

## Total dynamic head (TDH)

TDH is the resistance the pump must overcome, measured in feet of water column. Suction lift, pipe friction, and every valve, filter, and heater in the return path add to TDH. A pump's factory curve plots flow rate versus TDH: as TDH climbs, flow drops. When the field pressure is higher than the design pressure — a dirty filter, closed valve, or clogged skimmer basket — flow falls, turnover time stretches, and the sanitizer contact time drops.

## Pump selection and variable speed

Modern residential systems use variable-speed pumps almost universally because a pump running at half speed uses about one-eighth the energy per hour. The trade-off is longer runtime — half speed means half the flow, so the pump must run twice as long to achieve the same turnover. Variable-speed programming should always be verified with a flow meter; a nameplate horsepower rating is not a proxy for actual flow at the site's specific plumbing.

## Skimmers and main drains

Skimmers pull water off the surface where debris and body oils collect. A well-plumbed pool has 60–80 percent of its flow coming from skimmers and 20–40 percent from the main drain, with the balance adjustable through the equalizer valve. The main drain acts as insurance against skimmer entrapment and gives the pump a low-turbulence suction source when the water level is low.

## Virginia Graeme Baker (VGB) compliance

The Virginia Graeme Baker Pool and Spa Safety Act, enacted after the drowning death of a young child on an unprotected drain, requires every public pool in the United States to use suction fittings certified to ANSI/APSP/ICC-16, with either dual drains, an unblockable cover, or a safety vacuum release system. Residential retrofits are recommended and, in some jurisdictions, required. Whenever a technician replaces a drain cover, they must match the manufacturer, model, and installation orientation printed on the cover; a mismatched cover is treated by code as no cover at all.

## Return jet configuration

Return jets should be aimed to create a slow, unidirectional rotation of the entire pool. In a rectangular pool the jets are usually aimed slightly downward toward the far end so surface debris drifts toward the skimmer. In a freeform pool the technician must observe the water at low flow and adjust each jet by hand until the whole surface moves consistently. Dead zones — where you can drop a leaf and it does not move for a minute — will grow algae first.

## Cavitation and priming

Cavitation is what happens when the pump tries to move more water than the suction side can supply: dissolved air comes out of solution inside the impeller and collapses violently. It sounds like marbles in the pump housing and destroys impellers, seals, and even volutes over time. If a pump is cavitating, the fix is on the suction side — clear the basket, check for air leaks at unions and lid gaskets, and lower the pump speed until the noise goes away, then hunt the root cause.

## Field practice

Verify pump prime and quiet operation on every visit. Log filter pressure. Confirm both skimmers hold water and return-jet flow is even. Never remove a drain cover unless the pump is off at the disconnect. When plumbing new pipe, use flexible PVC only on straight runs and only where the manufacturer allows it — hard PVC is required in areas subject to sharp bends or freezing.
