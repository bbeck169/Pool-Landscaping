# Chapter 2 — Sanitization and Oxidation

*Original in-house authorship. External standards cited by name and link only.*

## Two jobs, one chemical

Chlorine performs two distinct jobs in a pool. The first is sanitization: killing pathogenic bacteria, viruses, and protozoa fast enough that bathers cannot infect each other. The second is oxidation: chemically destroying organic waste such as sweat, urine, sunscreen, and hair products before it becomes food for algae or reacts to form chloramines. A properly-run pool needs enough chlorine present to do both jobs continuously, and enough oxidation capacity in reserve to handle bather-load surges.

## Chlorine species and pH

When any chlorine product hits water it hydrolyzes into hypochlorous acid (HOCl) — the strongly-disinfecting form — and hypochlorite ion (OCl⁻) — a weakly-disinfecting form. The ratio between the two is controlled almost entirely by pH. At pH 7.0, water contains roughly 75 percent HOCl. At pH 7.5, it is close to 50/50. At pH 8.2, HOCl has fallen to about 15 percent, meaning most of the "chlorine" the test kit reads is doing very little sanitizing. This is why pool operators say "test kit results at pH 8 are lying to you" — the chlorine is present, but ineffective.

## Product forms

The industry uses several chlorine products interchangeably as raw sanitizer, but they are not equivalent from a chemistry standpoint. Sodium hypochlorite (liquid chlorine, 10–12.5 percent trade strength) is a high-pH product that adds no CYA. Calcium hypochlorite (cal-hypo, 65–73 percent) is high-pH and adds calcium hardness. Trichloroisocyanuric acid (trichlor, 90 percent as tablet or granular) is low-pH and adds CYA — every 10 ppm of chlorine added by trichlor also adds about 6 ppm of CYA. Dichloroisocyanurate (dichlor, granular) is roughly pH-neutral and also adds CYA. Choosing the wrong product for a specific pool problem is one of the most common field mistakes; understand the pH and side-effect profile of every product before you dose.

## Free, combined, and breakpoint

Free chlorine is the residual that has not yet reacted with anything. Combined chlorine is what remains after chlorine has bonded to nitrogen-containing waste — the notorious "chloramine smell" of an overloaded indoor pool. Chloramines are much weaker sanitizers than free chlorine and are strong irritants. To eliminate them, an operator must reach *breakpoint chlorination*: raising FAC to roughly ten times the combined chlorine level, which chemically destroys the chloramines and returns the residual to free form.

## Shocking versus superchlorination

The words "shock" and "superchlorination" are used loosely in the field, but they mean different things. Superchlorination is an intentional raise of FAC to about 5–10 ppm to burn off accumulated organics and low-level combined chlorine. Breakpoint shocking targets a higher FAC (10x CC) to eliminate a persistent chloramine or algae bloom. Neither is a substitute for good routine sanitation.

## Alternative sanitizer systems

Salt-chlorine generators (SCG) electrolyze dissolved salt (typically 3,000–3,500 ppm) into hypochlorous acid on-site. From the pool's perspective, the sanitizer is still chlorine — the difference is the delivery mechanism, not the chemistry. Bromine is used in spas and some indoor pools because it remains active at higher water temperatures. Supplemental oxidizers such as monopersulfate (non-chlorine shock), UV, and ozone destroy organics without adding chlorine but do not maintain a residual sanitizer; a chlorine or bromine base is still required.

## CYA and the "chlorine lock" myth

CYA does not "lock up" chlorine; it slows the free chlorine's ability to attack organics. Operators who chase FAC upward without addressing high CYA are treating a symptom. When CYA drifts above 100 ppm and combined chlorine will not fall, the answer is partial drain-and-refill, not doubling the shock dose. A modern rule of thumb keeps FAC at roughly 7.5 percent of CYA for baseline sanitation.

## Field practice

Always test pH before deciding on a chlorine dose — a high-pH pool needs pH correction first, chlorine second. Do not combine chlorine products in the same bucket, feeder, or storage cabinet. Never store cal-hypo next to trichlor; the accidental reaction is violent. Predissolve granular products and pour into the deep end with the pump running.
