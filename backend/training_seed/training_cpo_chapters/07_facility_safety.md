# Chapter 7 — Facility Safety

*Original in-house authorship. External standards cited by name and link only.*

## Two categories of risk

Every pool operator manages two very different categories of risk: chemical risk to the operator and staff, and physical risk to the swimming public. The two are managed differently, but both are subject to written procedures that a court will ask for after an incident. Chapter 7 covers the chemical and personal-safety side; drowning prevention is treated in the facility-management portion of Chapter 8.

## SDS binder — the anchor document

The Safety Data Sheet (SDS) for every chemical stored on the property must be accessible, current, and known to every person who might handle those chemicals. SDS sheets follow a 16-section format standardized under the Occupational Safety and Health Administration's Hazard Communication Standard. The most operationally-useful sections are Section 4 (first aid), Section 6 (accidental release), Section 7 (handling and storage), and Section 8 (exposure controls and PPE). A binder on the equipment pad or a QR-code-accessible database at the front desk both satisfy the "readily accessible" requirement — a locked drawer does not.

## Personal protective equipment

Baseline PPE for routine service includes chemical splash goggles, nitrile or neoprene gloves, closed-toe non-slip shoes, and long pants during any chemical handling. For dry-product handling — cal-hypo, calcium chloride flakes, DE — an N95 respirator prevents inhalation of caustic dust. For any confined-space or elevated concentration exposure — a chlorine gas leak, a fresh acid pour into a small pit — upgrade to a full-face respirator with an acid-gas cartridge, or clear the area entirely and let it ventilate before returning.

## Chemical storage separation

Chlorine oxidizers and acids must be stored physically separated — different rooms if possible, separate containment tubs at minimum. A drum of muriatic acid falling into a bucket of cal-hypo produces chlorine gas immediately and in dangerous volume. Store like with like: liquid chlorine on the left, all acids on the right, dry chlorines in a labeled ventilated cabinet, granular pH increaser on a shelf away from either. Never store any chemical directly on a bare concrete floor without secondary containment.

## Common accidental combinations

The two most common mistakes on the equipment pad both involve incompatible chlorine products. Cal-hypo and trichlor stored in the same container — even trace residues in an old scoop — will react exothermically and can ignite paper labels and plastic. Never re-use a scoop between products. Never add water to acid; add acid to water, and always into a large volume. Never mix bleach and ammonia — this is a domestic hazard that reaches pools when operators try to "clean" a cover with the wrong household cleaner.

## First aid basics

Every equipment room and pool office should have an eyewash station capable of 15 minutes of continuous flushing. Any chemical splash to the eye is a full 15-minute flush followed by immediate medical attention, no exceptions. Skin splashes: flush with copious water for at least 15 minutes and remove contaminated clothing before it holds the chemical against the skin. Chlorine gas inhalation: move to fresh air immediately; even mild exposures can develop into pulmonary edema several hours later and warrant medical evaluation.

## Slip, trip, and fall hazards

The deck around a pool is the highest-density source of workers-comp claims for pool operators. Non-slip surfaces, adequate lighting, and prompt removal of hoses, cords, and equipment during service reduce the incident rate dramatically. Wet chemical spills should be neutralized and rinsed to a floor drain, not left to evaporate; a dry crust of acid or chlorine on a walkway becomes a slip hazard once bathers wet it again.

## Electrical safety

All pool equipment must be bonded and grounded to the pool structure per the National Electrical Code Article 680. Never work on wet electrical components without cutting power at the disconnect and verifying dead with a meter, not by trusting the switch. GFCI protection is required on every 15A and 20A branch circuit within the pool equipment enclosure and on every receptacle within 20 feet of the water. When installing or servicing, if you find any receptacle without GFCI, note it in the report and recommend correction — an ungrounded pool circuit can and does kill.

## Lockout / tagout

Any time a pump, heater, or booster is being disassembled, apply a lockout on the disconnect and hang a personal tag. In a small residential setting, at minimum unplug the equipment and put the plug where you can see it while working. Assume that anyone who walks up while you are working will try to "help" by turning the equipment on for you.

## Field practice

Wear PPE every time — even the routine visits. Rotate SDS binder to reflect current inventory. Verify eyewash function monthly. When in doubt, evacuate and ventilate; there is no chemistry problem worth a hospital trip.
